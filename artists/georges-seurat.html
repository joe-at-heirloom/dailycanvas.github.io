Georges Seurat: paintings, life and where to see them

Georges Seurat (1859–1891) was a French painter who invented pointillism, building pictures from small dots of pure color. In a career of about ten years he founded Neo-Impressionism and painted A Sunday on La Grande Jatte.

Life

Seurat trained at the École des Beaux-Arts and studied the color theories of scientists such as Michel Eugène Chevreul. Before painting in color he made hundreds of drawings in black conté crayon, built from light and shadow rather than outlines.

His first large painting, Bathers at Asnières (1884), was rejected by the Salon. He spent the next two years on A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, shown at the last Impressionist exhibition in 1886, where Paul Signac and Camille Pissarro took up his method.

He painted harbors on the Channel coast each summer and, in his last years, night scenes of popular entertainment such as Circus Sideshow (1887–88). He died suddenly in 1891, at 31, probably of diphtheria or meningitis.

Where to see Seurat's work

The Art Institute of Chicago has A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, and the National Gallery in London has Bathers at Asnières. The Met, the Musée d'Orsay, the Barnes Foundation and the National Gallery of Art, which has many of his small oil studies, also have important works.

The paintings on this page: National Gallery of Art, Washington, DC (12); The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (6); Art Institute of Chicago (1); Cleveland Museum of Art (1).

Questions

What is pointillism?

Painting with small, separate dots of unmixed color that blend in the viewer's eye. Seurat developed it in the 1880s; he preferred the terms chromoluminarism and divisionism.

What is Seurat's most famous painting?

A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884–86), at the Art Institute of Chicago.

How long did Seurat spend on La Grande Jatte?

About two years, from 1884 to 1886, with dozens of studies. He added the painted border later, around 1888–89.
