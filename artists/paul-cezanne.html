Paul Cézanne: paintings, life and where to see them

Paul Cézanne (1839–1906) was a French Post-Impressionist painter who rebuilt painting from color and simple forms. His still lifes, card players and views of Mont Sainte-Victoire led directly to Cubism, and he is often called the father of modern art.

Life

The son of a banker in Aix-en-Provence, Cézanne grew up with the novelist Émile Zola, studied law to please his father, and went to Paris to paint in 1861. His early work was dark and violent, and the Salon rejected him again and again.

In the 1870s he painted outdoors with Camille Pissarro at Pontoise and showed at the first and third Impressionist exhibitions. Then he withdrew to Provence and worked mostly alone, painting apples, card players and the mountain near Aix, Mont Sainte-Victoire, dozens of times.

The dealer Ambroise Vollard gave him his first solo exhibition in 1895, and younger painters came to see him as a master. He caught a chill while painting outdoors in a storm in October 1906 and died a few days later in Aix.

Where to see Cézanne's work

The Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia has 69 of his paintings, the largest collection anywhere, and the Musée d'Orsay has many more. The Met, the National Gallery of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago and MoMA also have major works.

The paintings on this page: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (8); Art Institute of Chicago (7); National Gallery of Art, Washington, DC (4); Cleveland Museum of Art (1).

Questions

Why is Cézanne called the father of modern art?

Because he built pictures from patches of color and simple geometric forms rather than copying appearances, which led directly to Cubism. Picasso, Braque and Matisse all studied him closely.

How many versions of The Card Players are there?

Five, painted in the early 1890s. They range from large scenes with several players to small pictures of two men facing each other.

Where is the largest collection of Cézanne paintings?

The Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia, with 69.
