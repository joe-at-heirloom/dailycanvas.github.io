Camille Pissarro: paintings, life and where to see them

Camille Pissarro (1830–1903) was a Danish-French painter and the only artist to show in all eight Impressionist exhibitions. Generous and steady, he was a mentor to Cézanne and Gauguin and a father figure to the movement.

Life

Born on St. Thomas in the Caribbean, then a Danish colony, Pissarro went to school in Paris, worked in his family's store, and spent two years in Venezuela with a Danish painter before settling in Paris in 1855 to paint. Corot encouraged his landscapes.

He lived in the villages around Paris, Louveciennes and Pontoise, painting farms, roads and orchards. During the Franco-Prussian War he took refuge in London, as Monet did, and many paintings left in his house at Louveciennes were destroyed by soldiers billeted there.

In the mid-1880s he took up Seurat's dot technique for a few years. In his last years, troubled by an eye infection that kept him indoors, he painted series of Paris boulevards, Rouen and Le Havre from hotel windows. He died in Paris in 1903.

Where to see Pissarro's work

The Musée d'Orsay has a large collection, and the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford holds the Pissarro family archive. In the US, the Met, the National Gallery of Art and the Art Institute of Chicago have many of his paintings.

The paintings on this page: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (9); Art Institute of Chicago (8); National Gallery of Art, Washington, DC (3).

Questions

Which Impressionist showed in all eight exhibitions?

Camille Pissarro, the only one to show in every Impressionist exhibition from 1874 to 1886.

What are Pissarro's most famous paintings?

His views of Paris from hotel windows in the late 1890s, such as The Boulevard Montmartre on a Winter Morning (1897) at the Met, and rural scenes such as The Red Roofs (1877) at the Musée d'Orsay.

Who did Pissarro mentor?

He painted beside and advised Paul Cézanne at Pontoise in the 1870s, and encouraged Paul Gauguin when Gauguin was still a stockbroker.
