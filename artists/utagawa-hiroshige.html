Utagawa Hiroshige: prints, life and where to see them

Utagawa Hiroshige (1797–1858) was a Japanese printmaker, the great master of the landscape print. His scenes of rain, snow, moonlight and travel captivated Van Gogh, Monet and Whistler.

Life

Born Andō Tokutarō into a family of fire wardens at the shogun's castle in Edo, Hiroshige inherited his father's post as a boy. He studied under the print artist Utagawa Toyohiro, who gave him the name Hiroshige.

His series The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1833–34), views of the post towns on the highway between Edo and Kyoto, made him famous and sold in huge numbers. He went on to design thousands of prints, many of them in series of travel views.

His last great series, One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (1856–58), was still being published when he died in 1858, probably of cholera. Van Gogh copied two of its prints in oil in 1887.

Where to see Hiroshige's work

The Brooklyn Museum owns a complete set of One Hundred Famous Views of Edo. The Met, the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, the Art Institute of Chicago and the British Museum hold large collections of his prints, shown in rotation because they fade in light.

The prints on this page: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (19); Cleveland Museum of Art (1).

Questions

What is Hiroshige's most famous print?

Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake (1857), from One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, which Van Gogh copied in oil. As a series, The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō is the most famous.

Did Van Gogh copy Hiroshige?

Yes. In 1887 he painted copies of two prints from One Hundred Famous Views of Edo: Plum Park in Kameido and Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge. Both copies are in the Van Gogh Museum.

What is the difference between Hiroshige and Hokusai?

Both were great landscape printmakers of Edo. Hokusai, the elder, favored dramatic, stylized designs like The Great Wave; Hiroshige made quieter, atmospheric views of weather, seasons and travel.
