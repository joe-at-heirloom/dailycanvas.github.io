Mannerism: the paintings, the artists and how to recognize it

Mannerism is the style of Italian art from about 1520 to 1600, between the High Renaissance and the Baroque, when painters such as Pontormo, Parmigianino, Bronzino and El Greco traded natural balance for elongated figures, twisted poses and cool, unexpected color.

History

Mannerism takes its name from the Italian maniera, "style". It grew up in Florence and Rome in the 1520s, after Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael seemed to have perfected painting, and younger artists looked for ways to go beyond them.

Pontormo and Rosso Fiorentino in Florence, and Parmigianino in Parma and Rome, stretched bodies, twisted poses and used cool, acid colors. At the Medici court, Agnolo Bronzino painted portraits of polished, aloof elegance.

The style spread across Europe, to the French court at Fontainebleau and beyond. Its most original late voice was El Greco, who carried it to Spain and gave it a visionary intensity. By about 1600 Caravaggio and the Carracci had turned painting toward the drama and naturalism of the Baroque.

How to recognize it

Elongated figures and necks; strained poses; crowded or ambiguous space; polished, aloof court portraits.

Key artists: Pontormo, Parmigianino, Agnolo Bronzino, El Greco.

Questions

What is Mannerism in art?

A style of about 1520 to 1600, between the High Renaissance and the Baroque, that favored elegance and artifice over natural balance: elongated figures, twisted poses, crowded space and cool, unexpected color.

Who are the main Mannerist painters?

Pontormo, Rosso Fiorentino, Parmigianino and Agnolo Bronzino in Italy, and El Greco in Spain.

Why is it called Mannerism?

From the Italian maniera, meaning style or manner. Later critics used the word to dismiss art they found artificial; today it simply names the period.
