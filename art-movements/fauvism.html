Fauvism: the paintings, the artists and how to recognize it

Fauvism is the short-lived movement of about 1905 to 1908, led by Henri Matisse and André Derain, whose painters used pure, unnatural color to express feeling. A critic called them fauves, "wild beasts".

History

Fauvism was the first avant-garde movement of the 20th century, and one of the shortest. In the summer of 1905 at Collioure, on the Mediterranean, Henri Matisse and André Derain painted landscapes and portraits in pure, unmixed color straight from the tube.

At that autumn's Salon d'Automne in Paris, their pictures and those of Maurice de Vlaminck, Albert Marquet and others hung together in one room. The critic Louis Vauxcelles called the painters fauves, "wild beasts", and the name stuck.

Raoul Dufy, Othon Friesz, Kees van Dongen and the young Georges Braque joined them, but by 1908 most had moved on, Braque to Cubism. The Fauves' discovery, color freed from describing things, became a foundation of modern art.

How to recognize it

Pure, clashing color straight from the tube; green faces and red trees; loose, simplified drawing.

Key artists: Henri Matisse, André Derain, Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy.

Questions

What is Fauvism?

A movement of about 1905 to 1908, led by Henri Matisse and André Derain, that used intense, unnatural color to express feeling and build pictures, rather than to describe things.

Who were the Fauves?

Henri Matisse, André Derain, Maurice de Vlaminck, Albert Marquet, Raoul Dufy, Othon Friesz, Kees van Dongen and, briefly, Georges Braque.

Why were they called wild beasts?

At the 1905 Salon d'Automne the critic Louis Vauxcelles saw a classical-looking sculpture in the room among their violently colored canvases and called it Donatello among the wild beasts, "Donatello chez les fauves".
