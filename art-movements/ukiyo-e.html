Ukiyo-e: Japanese woodblock prints, the artists and the history

Ukiyo-e, "pictures of the floating world", are the Japanese woodblock prints of the Edo period: actors, beautiful women and, later, landscapes by artists such as Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige and Kitagawa Utamaro.

History

Ukiyo-e means "pictures of the floating world": the pleasure districts, kabuki theaters and fashions of Edo, now Tokyo, under the Tokugawa shoguns. It began in the late 17th century with the black-and-white prints and illustrated books of Hishikawa Moronobu.

Prints were a collaboration: a publisher commissioned the design from an artist, a carver cut a woodblock for each color, and a printer pressed them onto paper by hand. Full-color prints, called nishiki-e or "brocade pictures", appeared in 1765 with Suzuki Harunobu. Kitagawa Utamaro made celebrated portraits of beautiful women, and Tōshūsai Sharaku produced his striking actor portraits within about ten months in 1794–95.

In the 1830s Katsushika Hokusai and Utagawa Hiroshige made landscape a major subject, with Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji and The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō. After Japan opened to trade in the 1850s, prints reached Europe in quantity and inspired Monet, Degas, Van Gogh, Whistler and Cassatt.

How to recognize it

Flat areas of color; strong outlines; bold cropping and unusual viewpoints; rain, snow and Mount Fuji.

Key artists: Hishikawa Moronobu, Kitagawa Utamaro, Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige.

Questions

What does ukiyo-e mean?

"Pictures of the floating world": the world of pleasure, theater and fashion in Edo-period Japan.

How were ukiyo-e prints made?

An artist drew the design, a carver cut it into blocks of cherry wood, one for each color, and a printer inked the blocks and pressed paper onto them by hand. A publisher organized and sold the edition.

Who are the most famous ukiyo-e artists?

Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige, Kitagawa Utamaro, Tōshūsai Sharaku, Suzuki Harunobu and Utagawa Kuniyoshi.
