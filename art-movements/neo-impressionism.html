Pointillism and Neo-Impressionism: the paintings, the artists and the technique

Pointillism is the technique of painting in small dots of pure color that blend in the viewer's eye, invented by Georges Seurat in the 1880s. The movement built on it, led by Seurat and Paul Signac, is called Neo-Impressionism.

History

Pointillism was invented by Georges Seurat in the mid-1880s. Drawing on the color theories of the chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul and the physicist Ogden Rood, he painted in small dots of pure color placed side by side, so they would mix in the viewer's eye rather than on the palette.

A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, shown at the last Impressionist exhibition in 1886, launched the movement, and the critic Félix Fénéon named it Neo-Impressionism. Paul Signac became its spokesman and theorist, and Camille Pissarro used the technique for several years.

After Seurat's early death in 1891, Signac and Henri-Edmond Cross loosened the dots into larger, mosaic-like strokes of brilliant color, which in turn inspired Henri Matisse and the Fauves in the early 1900s.

How to recognize it

Even dots or dashes of unmixed color; still, carefully composed scenes; shimmering, luminous surfaces.

Key artists: Georges Seurat, Paul Signac, Henri-Edmond Cross, Maximilien Luce.

Questions

What is pointillism?

A technique of painting in small, separate dots of pure color that blend in the viewer's eye. Georges Seurat developed it in the 1880s.

What is the difference between pointillism and Impressionism?

Impressionists painted quickly and by eye to catch a passing effect of light. Pointillists worked slowly and methodically, placing dots of color according to scientific theories of color.

What is the most famous pointillist painting?

Georges Seurat's A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884–86), at the Art Institute of Chicago.
