Art movements: a timeline from the Renaissance to Pop Art

An art movement is a style or approach shared by a group of artists at a particular time and place. The major movements in Western painting, in order, are the Renaissance, Mannerism, Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Symbolism, Art Nouveau, Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism, Futurism, Dada, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism and Pop Art. Below is a timeline of 27 movements with their dates, where they happened, the artists who defined them, how to recognize them and public-domain examples.

The Renaissance

c. 1400–1600 · Italy

A rebirth of the art and learning of ancient Greece and Rome that began in Florence in the early 1400s. Painters used linear perspective, anatomy and oil glazes to build convincing space and lifelike bodies, and the High Renaissance of Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael around 1500 set the standard for European art for centuries.

Key artists: Masaccio, Sandro Botticelli, Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Raphael, Titian.

Artist pages: Sandro Botticelli.

How to recognize it: Balanced, symmetrical compositions; one-point perspective; idealized figures; classical architecture and myth alongside Christian subjects.

The Northern Renaissance

c. 1430–1580 · Flanders, the Netherlands and Germany

North of the Alps, painters perfected oil paint and minute detail. Their pictures are full of closely observed things, from fabrics and reflections to winter landscapes, and often carry hidden symbolism.

Key artists: Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Hieronymus Bosch, Albrecht Dürer, Hans Holbein the Younger, Pieter Bruegel the Elder.

How to recognize it: Jewel-like detail and texture; deep, glowing color from layered oil glazes; everyday objects charged with meaning.

Mannerism

c. 1520–1600 · Italy, then Spain and northern Europe

After the High Renaissance, artists in Florence and Rome deliberately bent its rules. Bodies grew long and twisted, color turned cool or acidic, and elegance and artifice mattered more than natural balance.

Key artists: Pontormo, Parmigianino, Agnolo Bronzino, El Greco.

Artist pages: El Greco.

How to recognize it: Elongated figures and necks; strained poses; crowded or ambiguous space; polished, aloof court portraits.

Baroque

c. 1600–1750 · Rome, then Catholic Europe

Born in Rome around 1600 and spread by the Catholic Church and royal courts, Baroque art aims for drama and emotion. Scenes are caught at their most intense moment and lit like a stage.

Key artists: Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, Peter Paul Rubens, Gian Lorenzo Bernini, Diego Velázquez.

Artist pages: Caravaggio.

How to recognize it: Chiaroscuro, sharp light against deep shadow; diagonal, swirling compositions; rich color and theatrical gesture.

The Dutch Golden Age

c. 1600–1680 · The Dutch Republic

In the newly independent, Protestant Dutch Republic, painters sold to merchants rather than the church, and specialized in portraits, landscapes, still lifes and everyday interiors. The best of them made ordinary moments feel timeless.

Key artists: Rembrandt, Frans Hals, Johannes Vermeer, Jacob van Ruisdael, Jan Steen.

Artist pages: Johannes Vermeer.

How to recognize it: Quiet domestic scenes; soft window light; small canvases made for private homes; brilliant still lifes and wide, low-horizon landscapes.

Ukiyo-e

c. 1670s–1900 · Japan

Woodblock prints of the "floating world" of Edo, now Tokyo: actors, courtesans and, later, landscapes and travel views. Cheap and mass-produced, they reached Europe in the 1850s and 60s and deeply influenced the Impressionists and Van Gogh.

Key artists: Hishikawa Moronobu, Kitagawa Utamaro, Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige.

Artist pages: Katsushika Hokusai, Utagawa Hiroshige.

How to recognize it: Flat areas of color; strong outlines; bold cropping and unusual viewpoints; rain, snow and Mount Fuji.

Rococo

c. 1720–1780 · France, then Europe

A lighter, playful offshoot of the Baroque that took hold in Paris after the death of Louis XIV in 1715. Its subjects are love, leisure and flirtation in parks and boudoirs.

Key artists: Antoine Watteau, François Boucher, Jean-Honoré Fragonard, Giovanni Battista Tiepolo.

How to recognize it: Pastel colors; curving, decorative forms; feathery brushwork; aristocrats at play.

Neoclassicism

c. 1760–1830 · France, then Europe and America

A reaction against Rococo frivolity, inspired by the excavations of Pompeii and Herculaneum and by Enlightenment ideals. Painters told stories of civic virtue and sacrifice from ancient Rome in a clean, sober style.

Key artists: Jacques-Louis David, Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, Angelica Kauffman, Antonio Canova.

How to recognize it: Crisp outlines and smooth, invisible brushwork; calm, frieze-like compositions; Roman architecture and costume.

Romanticism

c. 1790–1850 · Europe

Romantic painters put feeling, imagination and the overwhelming power of nature ahead of reason and rules. Storms, shipwrecks, revolutions and lone figures before vast landscapes are its subjects.

Key artists: Francisco Goya, Caspar David Friedrich, J. M. W. Turner, John Constable, Théodore Géricault, Eugène Delacroix.

Artist pages: Francisco Goya, J. M. W. Turner.

How to recognize it: Dramatic skies and light; loose, energetic brushwork; the sublime in nature; heroism, terror and longing.

The Hudson River School

c. 1825–1875 · United States

America's first homegrown school of painting: large, luminous landscapes of the Hudson Valley and the Catskills, later of the American West and South America, painted as a celebration of a wild continent.

Key artists: Thomas Cole, Asher B. Durand, Frederic Edwin Church, Albert Bierstadt.

Artist pages: Thomas Cole, Albert Bierstadt.

How to recognize it: Panoramic views; glowing, golden light; tiny human figures dwarfed by mountains, falls and sky.

Realism

c. 1840–1880 · France, then Europe and America

Realists rejected idealized history and myth to paint ordinary people and the world as it was, including hard labor and poverty. Gustave Courbet led the movement in France and insisted on painting only what he could see.

Key artists: Gustave Courbet, Jean-François Millet, Honoré Daumier, Rosa Bonheur, Winslow Homer.

Artist pages: Winslow Homer, John Singer Sargent, Édouard Manet.

How to recognize it: Peasants, workers and everyday life at full scale; earthy colors; no idealizing or moralizing gloss.

The Pre-Raphaelites

1848–c. 1900 · England

A brotherhood of young English painters, founded in 1848, who wanted to return to the vivid color and detail of art before Raphael. Their later work, led by Rossetti and Burne-Jones, drifted toward dreamy medieval and literary subjects.

Key artists: Dante Gabriel Rossetti, John Everett Millais, William Holman Hunt, Edward Burne-Jones.

How to recognize it: Saturated jewel colors; sharp detail in every leaf; long-haired women; subjects from Dante, Shakespeare and Arthurian legend.

Impressionism

c. 1867–1886 · France

A group of Paris painters, shut out of the official Salon, held eight exhibitions of their own from 1874 to 1886. They painted quickly, often outdoors, to catch changing light and modern life: cafés, boating parties, railway stations and city streets.

Key artists: Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Edgar Degas, Camille Pissarro, Berthe Morisot, Alfred Sisley, Mary Cassatt.

Artist pages: Édouard Manet, Edgar Degas, Camille Pissarro, Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Berthe Morisot, Mary Cassatt.

How to recognize it: Short, visible brushstrokes; bright, unblended color; colored shadows; everyday modern subjects and open-air light.

Symbolism

c. 1880–1910 · France, Belgium and across Europe

Symbolists turned away from the visible world toward dreams, myth, death and the inner life, using images as symbols of ideas and moods rather than records of things seen.

Key artists: Gustave Moreau, Odilon Redon, Arnold Böcklin, Fernand Khnopff, Edvard Munch.

How to recognize it: Mysterious, often eerie scenes; femmes fatales and sphinxes; twilight and silence.

Neo-Impressionism (pointillism)

c. 1886–1910 · France

Georges Seurat replaced the Impressionists' instinct with color science, building pictures from small dots of pure color meant to blend in the viewer's eye. The method is known as pointillism or divisionism.

Key artists: Georges Seurat, Paul Signac, Henri-Edmond Cross, Maximilien Luce.

Artist pages: Camille Pissarro, Georges Seurat.

How to recognize it: Even dots or dashes of unmixed color; still, carefully composed scenes; shimmering, luminous surfaces.

Post-Impressionism

c. 1886–1905 · France

Not a single style but the artists who took Impressionism's bright color in personal directions: Cézanne toward structure, Van Gogh toward emotion, Gauguin toward flat color and symbol. The English critic Roger Fry coined the name in 1910.

Key artists: Paul Cézanne, Vincent van Gogh, Paul Gauguin, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec.

Artist pages: Paul Cézanne, Paul Gauguin, Vincent van Gogh.

How to recognize it: Bold, expressive color; thick or patterned brushwork; simplified, solid forms; personal vision over optical accuracy.

Art Nouveau and the Vienna Secession

c. 1890–1914 · Paris, Brussels, Vienna and beyond

An international decorative style of flowing, plant-like lines that ran from posters and architecture into painting. In Vienna, Klimt and others broke away from the academy in 1897 to found the Secession, whose art mixes gold, ornament and symbolism.

Key artists: Gustav Klimt, Alphonse Mucha, Koloman Moser, Aubrey Beardsley.

Artist pages: Gustav Klimt.

How to recognize it: Whiplash curves; flat ornamental patterns; gold leaf; stylized women and flowers.

Fauvism

c. 1905–1908 · France

At the 1905 Salon d'Automne in Paris a critic called Matisse and his friends fauves, "wild beasts", for their violent, unnatural color. Color was set free from describing things and used for feeling and structure.

Key artists: Henri Matisse, André Derain, Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy.

Artist pages: Henri Matisse.

How to recognize it: Pure, clashing color straight from the tube; green faces and red trees; loose, simplified drawing.

Expressionism

c. 1905–1925 · Germany and Austria

Expressionists distorted form and color to convey emotion and anxiety rather than appearance. Its two main groups were Die Brücke (The Bridge), founded in Dresden in 1905, and Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider), formed in Munich in 1911.

Key artists: Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Emil Nolde, Franz Marc, Wassily Kandinsky, Egon Schiele.

How to recognize it: Jagged, angular forms; harsh or symbolic color; raw brushwork; city streets, nudes, animals and spiritual themes.

Cubism

c. 1907–1920s · Paris

Picasso and Braque broke objects into facets and showed them from several viewpoints at once, then began gluing paper and newsprint into their pictures. It opened the way to most of the abstract art that followed. Picasso's and Braque's own works are still under copyright, so the examples here are by Gris and Léger.

Key artists: Pablo Picasso, Georges Braque, Juan Gris, Fernand Léger, Robert Delaunay.

How to recognize it: Fragmented, overlapping planes; muted browns and grays at first, brighter later; guitars, bottles, newspapers and café tables.

Futurism

1909–c. 1916 · Italy

Launched by the poet F. T. Marinetti's manifesto in 1909, Futurism celebrated speed, machines and the modern city, and rejected the past. Its painters showed movement as repeated, overlapping forms.

Key artists: Umberto Boccioni, Giacomo Balla, Gino Severini, Carlo Carrà.

How to recognize it: Blurred, repeated shapes suggesting motion; lines of force; cars, trains, crowds and electric light.

Works from this movement are still under copyright, so none are shown here.

Suprematism and Constructivism

c. 1915–1930 · Russia

Kazimir Malevich reduced painting to pure geometric shapes, unveiling Black Square in 1915. After the 1917 revolution, Constructivists such as Popova and Rodchenko turned abstraction toward design, posters and building a new society.

Key artists: Kazimir Malevich, Lyubov Popova, El Lissitzky, Alexander Rodchenko.

How to recognize it: Flat geometric forms floating on white; squares, bars and wedges; later, typography and industrial design.

Dada

c. 1916–1924 · Zurich, Berlin, Paris and New York

Founded at the Cabaret Voltaire in Zurich in 1916 in disgust at the First World War, Dada mocked art itself with nonsense, collage, chance and readymades, such as Marcel Duchamp's urinal, Fountain (1917).

Key artists: Marcel Duchamp, Hannah Höch, Jean Arp, Kurt Schwitters, Man Ray.

How to recognize it: Collage and photomontage; found objects; absurd titles; deliberate provocation.

Works from this movement are still under copyright, so none are shown here.

De Stijl

1917–1931 · The Netherlands

A Dutch group named after its magazine, De Stijl (The Style), that reduced art to straight lines, right angles and the primary colors with black, white and gray. Mondrian called his version Neo-Plasticism.

Key artists: Piet Mondrian, Theo van Doesburg, Gerrit Rietveld, Bart van der Leck.

How to recognize it: Black grids; flat rectangles of red, yellow and blue; only horizontals and verticals, until van Doesburg began tilting his grids in the mid-1920s.

Surrealism

c. 1924–1950s · Paris, then worldwide

Founded in Paris with André Breton's 1924 manifesto, Surrealism tried to unlock the unconscious through dream imagery, automatic drawing and chance. Its best-known painting is Salvador Dalí's The Persistence of Memory (1931), with its melting watches.

Key artists: Salvador Dalí, René Magritte, Max Ernst, Joan Miró, Leonora Carrington.

How to recognize it: Dreamlike, impossible scenes painted with realistic precision; strange juxtapositions; or loose, automatic doodles and biomorphic shapes.

Works from this movement are still under copyright, so none are shown here.

Abstract Expressionism

c. 1943–1960 · New York

The first American movement to lead world art, centered in New York after the Second World War: huge canvases of gestural paint, like Jackson Pollock's drips, or fields of color, like Mark Rothko's glowing rectangles.

Key artists: Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Mark Rothko, Lee Krasner, Barnett Newman.

How to recognize it: Wall-sized canvases; drips, splashes and slashing brushwork; or vast, quiet areas of color.

Works from this movement are still under copyright, so none are shown here.

Pop Art

c. 1955–1970 · Britain and the United States

Pop artists borrowed from advertising, comic strips and supermarket shelves, as in Andy Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans (1962) and Roy Lichtenstein's blown-up comic panels.

Key artists: Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Richard Hamilton, Claes Oldenburg.

How to recognize it: Bright commercial colors; celebrities and brand names; screen printing, Ben-Day dots and repetition.

Works from this movement are still under copyright, so none are shown here.

Questions

What are the major art movements in order?

In Western painting: the Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), Mannerism (c. 1520–1600), Baroque (c. 1600–1750), Rococo (c. 1720–1780), Neoclassicism (c. 1760–1830), Romanticism (c. 1790–1850), Realism (c. 1840–1880), Impressionism (c. 1867–1886), Post-Impressionism (c. 1886–1905), Symbolism (c. 1880–1910), Art Nouveau (c. 1890–1914), Fauvism (c. 1905–1908), Expressionism (c. 1905–1925), Cubism (c. 1907–1920s), Futurism (1909–c. 1916), Dada (c. 1916–1924), Surrealism (c. 1924–1950s), Abstract Expressionism (c. 1943–1960) and Pop Art (c. 1955–1970). The dates overlap, and art historians draw the edges differently.

What is the difference between Impressionism and Post-Impressionism?

Impressionists such as Monet and Renoir tried to record a fleeting moment of light and color as the eye sees it. Post-Impressionists such as Cézanne, Van Gogh and Gauguin kept the bright palette but used it for structure, emotion or symbolism, moving away from what the eye sees toward a personal vision.

What art movement was Van Gogh part of?

Vincent van Gogh is a Post-Impressionist. He absorbed Impressionism and Japanese prints in Paris in 1886–88, then developed his own style of thick, expressive brushwork and intense color in Arles and Saint-Rémy. His work strongly influenced Fauvism and Expressionism.

What is the difference between Baroque and Rococo?

Baroque art (c. 1600–1750) is grand, dramatic and often religious, with deep shadows and strong emotion. Rococo (c. 1720–1780) grew out of it in France and is lighter and more intimate: pastel colors, playful curves and scenes of love and leisure for aristocratic homes.

Why are there no pictures for Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism or Pop Art?

Most works from those movements are still protected by copyright. Every image on this page is in the public domain, which is also why each one can be downloaded free as a wallpaper.
